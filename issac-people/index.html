Part two

How to read this file

Four rules, and the one rule that governs the whole document.

Every operator below was read the same way, on the same day: profile, headline, follower count where LinkedIn showed it, then the last five to six posts on the activity feed with the reaction, comment and repost counts recorded exactly as displayed. Then every other channel that person publishes on, verified at source. No estimates, no ranges, no “approximately”. Where a number is missing it is because LinkedIn did not show it.

  1. The numbers are small on purpose
    A post at 48 reactions looks like nothing next to a marketing influencer. In this niche it is the top of the market. Read every number against the others on this page, never against LinkedIn in general.
  2. Reactions measure reach. Comments measure nerve.
    A post at 20 reactions and 16 comments beat a post at 45 reactions and 4 comments, for our purposes. The comment is the thing that opens a conversation. Issac sells conversations, not impressions.
  3. A hook is a mechanic, not a subject
    “THE DAY AFTER” is a territory — a thing to talk about. “Every year I talk with owners who tell me the same thing” is a framework — a way to open. Twelve territories times twelve frameworks is one hundred and forty-four openings, which is why this file does not need to touch the territory list.
  4. Steal the structure, never the sentence
    Every framework in part seven is written as a template with the specifics removed. The example lines underneath are drafts in Issac's territory, not lines to publish. They get rewritten in his real voice the day the Gmail file lands.

Part six

Twelve hook frameworks

The money section. Each one is a template, why it works, and a shape test in his territory.

Twelve opening mechanics, lifted structure-first from the posts above. Each one is the template with the specifics stripped out, why it works on this reader, and a draft line in Issac's territory. The draft lines are not copy. They are shape tests. Every one gets rewritten in his real voice the day the Gmail file lands. These frameworks sit underneath the twelve hook territories already in the strategy — a territory is what to talk about, a framework is how to open it.

Framework 01

The regulation clock

Stolen from: Scot Cockroft — 48 reactions, his best

“[Rule / threshold / filing] changes on [date]. Here is what it means if you [own / are selling / hold the property].” Then three plain lines of consequence. No opinion.

It is the only kind of post that is useful to forward. A dated fact with a deadline creates the one thing insight posts never do: a reason to send it to a partner today. It also positions the writer as the person who reads the paperwork, which is exactly what a seller wants in a broker.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyTREC changed the disclosure rule on 1 September. If your building is part of the sale, here is the one line in your listing agreement that decides whether the buyer can walk in year two.
Framework 02

The tenure claim with an admission

Stolen from: Scot Cockroft — second-best post

“After [N] years of [doing the thing], I've become convinced of something: [bold claim]. That's a bold statement. But I've watched it happen over and over.”

The admission line is the mechanic. Naming your own claim as bold before the reader can dismiss it removes the reader's escape route — they cannot accuse you of overreach because you already did. The tenure number does the rest of the work.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyThirty-one years in the same building, and I have become convinced of something: most owners in this county sell the business and give the property away for free. That is a bold thing to say. I have watched it happen more times than I can count.
Framework 03

The adjacent-profession challenge

Stolen from: Elliott Holland — 20 reactions, 16 comments, best ratio in the sample

“Your [role the reader also employs] is not a [what they assume]. They're a [what they actually are]. Ask them [specific question]. The answer is almost always [the uncomfortable answer].”

It forces a side. The reader either defends their CPA or quietly checks. Both reactions are comments. This is the only mechanic in the sample that reliably generates a conversation rather than a thumb.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyYour CPA is not a valuation professional. They are a compliance professional. Ask them how many businesses they have taken to market. The answer is almost always zero. Rule: the target is always a role, never a named person or firm.
Framework 04

The internal monologue in quotes

Stolen from: Jessica Fialkovich — 76 reactions, the niche ceiling. Issac reacted to it himself

Open with the exact sentence the reader says to himself, in quotation marks, and nothing else. Then take it apart in four or five short lines.

There is no setup to scroll past. The first thing the reader sees is his own thought, which means the stop is involuntary. It works because it is not addressed to him — it is him.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copy“Maybe next year.” I have heard it every January for thirty-one years. The owners who said it in 2019 are the ones I am still talking to.
Framework 05

The regret quote

Stolen from: Jeff Adam — 25 reactions, his best. Independently confirms THE DAY AFTER

“Every year I talk with [reader type] who tell me the same thing: '[the regret, in their words].' Not because [obvious reason]. But because [the real reason].”

Two moves in one. The recurring quote makes the writer the person who has heard it a hundred times. The not-because / but-because turn takes away the reader's comfortable explanation and replaces it with the uncomfortable one.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyEvery year an owner tells me the same thing: I wish I had done this sooner. Not because he needed the money. Because he waited, and the tenant left, and the building went from an asset to a problem.
Framework 06

The withheld detail on a real document

Stolen from: Christine McDannell — 45 reactions, 10 comments

“There was ONE line in [the specific document] that gave the whole thing away.” Then the situation, then the line, then what it cost.

A document is a physical object, which makes the curiosity gap concrete rather than rhetorical. The capitalised word does the rest — one word per post, never two.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyThere was one line in the offer letter that told me the buyer was never going to close. It was on page four and it had nothing to do with the price.
Framework 07

The decoded phrase

Stolen from: Rochelle O'Brien — her highest comment ratio

“Ever had someone say '[industry phrase]' and felt [the physical reaction]? Turns out that's not actually [what you assumed]. It's [what it really is].”

It rewards the reader for something that already happened to him. He has heard the phrase across a table and never asked what it meant. Being told, three years later, produces a comment.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyEver had a buyer say he needs to run it past his partners, and felt the room go cold? That is almost never a no. It is a price move, and there is one sentence that answers it.
Framework 08

The character with a place and a contradiction

Stolen from: Clint Fiore — 64 reactions, highest in Texas

An unnamed but specific person, a named place, and one thing about them that does not fit. Then what happened.

Specificity without identification. The reader believes it because the details are too odd to invent, and nobody is exposed because there is no name.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyA man walked into my office in 1998 with a printing business and a building he had bought for $40,000. He wanted to sell the business and keep the building. He had it exactly backwards. Constraint: public-record or freely-told business facts only. Never the banned list.
Framework 09

The checklist reorder

Stolen from: Adam Coffey — the prose standard

“Before you look at [the obvious first thing], look at [the unobvious thing]. That decides [what is really at stake].”

It does not add information, it reorders what the reader already has. Reordering is cheaper to write and lands harder than teaching, because the reader recognises every item on the list.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyBefore you look at what the business is worth, look at who owns the building. That decides whether you are selling a company or a lease.
Framework 10

The actual price list

Stolen from: Adam Coffey

“People assume [the expensive part] is [X]. Here is the actual price list.” Then real numbers, in dollars, one per line.

Concrete figures replace adjectives. It is the fastest way to sound like someone who has actually done it, and it is unbluffable — either you know the numbers or you do not.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyOwners assume the expensive part of selling is the commission. Here is the actual price list. A valuation done off a tax return: free, and roughly thirty percent low.
Framework 11

The bias reveal

Stolen from: Christine McDannell

“[Thing the reader is drawn to] is almost always the most tempting. NOT because it is [the good reason]. Because [the human reason].”

It names a mistake the reader is currently making without accusing him, because the cause given is fatigue or hope rather than stupidity. That is why it produces agreement instead of defence.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyThe first offer is almost always the most tempting one. Not because it is the best. Because it is the first, and by then the owner is tired of thinking about it.
Framework 12

The numbered list with the stake on the last item

Stolen from: Elliott Holland — 15 reactions

“There are [N] steps to [the thing]. Step [N] is the only one keeping you out of [the catastrophe].”

A list promise plus jeopardy. The reader has to reach the end to find out which step matters, and the count in the first line tells him exactly how long that will take.

Shape test in Issac's territory — not final copyThere are four documents a buyer will ask for before he makes an offer. The fourth one is the only one that decides the price.

Twelve frameworks against twelve territories. Every framework works on more than one territory and every territory can be opened by more than one framework. That is one hundred and forty-four possible openings against a bank of forty-eight posts, which means the hook test never runs out of shapes and never has to change its subject matter. Nothing in the existing strategy moves.

Part ten

What this file cannot tell you

Five limits, stated before anyone quotes a number from it.

  • This is a snapshot of one day — Every number was read on 7 September 2026 from the last five or six posts on each activity feed. It is a recent-form reading, not a history. Nobody's best-ever post is in here unless it happened to be recent.
  • Reaction counts are not reach — LinkedIn does not show impressions on someone else's post. A 48-reaction post from a 3,000-follower account and a 48 from a 30,000-follower account mean very different things, and we cannot tell them apart from outside.
  • Follower counts were only visible for some — Where LinkedIn displayed a follower count it is on the page. Where it did not, there is no number and we did not estimate one.
  • None of this proves revenue — Not one of these twelve people has published what their content earns. Engagement is the only thing measured here. The link between a post and a signed listing agreement is unproven for every single operator on this page, including the ones at the top.
  • The sample is small because the niche is small — Twelve is not a survey. It is close to the complete set of individual seller-side operators who post regularly and can be found by search in this segment. The thinness of the field is itself the finding.
