What Issac actually does

Read this first. Everything else depends on it.

Issac is a business broker. Somebody owns a company and wants out — they are old, tired, sick, or their kids do not want it. Issac takes that company to market, finds a buyer, and gets paid a percentage when the sale closes. Roughly 10% on the business and 3–6% on the property.

The only thing that counts as a win: a signed listing agreement

Until an owner signs a listing agreement, Issac has nothing. A friendly conversation is worth zero. A listing agreement is an exclusive right to sell that company for a fixed period — it is the moment the deal becomes his.

That signature is the product we are manufacturing. The list, the domains, the sequences, the CRM — all of it exists to produce signatures on that one page. This is why "we sent 4,000 emails" is not a result and "he signed" is.

Why an owner who owns his own building is worth double

Two things get sold, not one: the company (customers, contracts, staff, machines, the name) and the dirt (the land and the building). If the operating company owns its own building, one person signs for both and Issac earns a fee on each.

If the family holds the land personally and the company just rents it, you need two agreements, two sets of expectations, and eventually two sets of heirs. Those deals take about a year longer, and often never happen. This is the single filter that took the candidate pool from 17 down to 5.

The two county files: COMMERCIAL and BPP

Dallas County keeps two separate records on a business. COMMERCIAL is the real property — the land and the buildings. BPP is Business Personal Property — the machines, tooling, racking and inventory bolted to the floor.

A company with a BPP account and no commercial account is a tenant in its own factory. All five names here hold both accounts, in the operating company's own name. That is what "verified" means on this list.

Why a 60-year-old business is the target and a 5-year-old one is not

Age is a proxy for four things at once: the owner is old, the customer list is real, the machinery is paid for, and the question "what happens to this when I stop" is live in the owner's head right now. A young business has no answer to sell.

Why the equipment number matters more than it looks

$11.4M of machines is not just a big number. It is (a) the reason a buyer buys the whole company instead of starting his own, because replacing that is slower and more expensive than buying it, and (b) a floor under the price — nobody sells a company for less than the resale value of what is bolted to the floor.

Why the land can be worth more than the business sitting on it

At Cary Products, 16 acres of light industrial ground is assessed at $1,395,924 and every building on it together is worth $931,430. The buildings are almost noise. Somebody buying that site may not want a plastics company at all — they want a distribution site eleven miles from downtown Dallas.

Two different buyers, two different prices. A broker who prices it as "a plastics company" leaves the bigger number on the table.

The property tax bill is a free opening line

Garland Steel pays $85,455.62 a year in property tax whether they have a good year or a terrible one. The owner feels that number every year, and it is public record. "You're carrying eighty-five thousand a year on that building" is an opening that is about his money, not ours.

The timing reality

Issac's own cycle is two to ten months from first contact to close. The 30-day build cannot produce a closing — it produces conversations and signed listing agreements. First realistic money from work started today lands around February 2027. Say that out loud to him before Day 30, not after.

03

Your succession question

You were right, and the deck currently argues the opposite.
What you asked

"There has been a succession — the father gave it to the son, and now the son is the CEO. What stops the same thing happening again? Nothing tells us this guy will not hand the company to his own son."

Nothing does stop it. And the ready list reads that fact backwards.

The deck's line on Republic is that the succession question "has already been answered once in this family, and everyone knows what it looked like" — written as a point in its favour. Your instinct is the correct one: a family that has transferred four times has demonstrated that its answer to "what happens next" is transfer, not sale. That is a pattern, not a one-off event.

His own ICP document already says this in plain language. The exclusion criterion is "a family succession plan in place = not a seller", and one of his named sale-readiness signals is "limited or no succession plan." The deck's copy contradicts the client's own written brief. If he reads it closely he will notice.

Fix before this list is referenced again

Rewrite the "why it scores" lines for Republic and Cary. Right now both use the next generation already being seated as evidence of readiness. Under his ICP, that is the disqualifier — not the qualifier. Everything else on the page is verified public record and stands.

The distinction that actually predicts a sale

It is not how many generations there have been. Almost every 60-year-old industrial business in Dallas County is a family business — if generation count were the filter, there would be no list. The number that predicts a sale is whether there is a named next-generation family member employed in the business right now.

  • Cary Products — closed. Earl Armstrong III is Chairman. Earl Armstrong IV is Chief Operating Officer. The father has moved up and the son is running operations. That is a handover mid-execution.
  • Southern Maid — closed. Second and third generations, in ownership and in management, published by the company itself.
  • Garland Steel — probably closed. William Becker is President; Josh Becker "also holds a role." Same surname, same building. Unconfirmed but likely.
  • Republic Sheet Metal — unknown, and leaning open. Zach Goff is generation four. There is no generation five named anywhere — and a 64-year-old company that publishes its own history page would almost certainly say so if there were.
  • Thermo Sensors — open. One named principal. No family names on any record. The only clean one.

There is also a real argument in Republic's favour, and it is worth knowing because it is the counter to your own question: family businesses run out of heirs. Roughly a third reach a second generation, around one in eight reach a third, and only a few percent reach a fourth. Republic is already in that last group. Reaching a fifth is rare enough that the base rate now works for a sale rather than against it. Zach Goff came into the business in 1992, which puts him somewhere in his fifties or sixties — squarely inside Issac's 55–72 sweet spot, and squarely in the years when this decision gets made.

So Republic is not disqualified. It is unresolved — which is exactly why it scores 12 out of 25 there and not 5 or 20.

The cheapest fix in the whole build

None of this can be settled from a database. It is settled by one question on the first call, and it should be the first question, before financials and before timeline:

"Who takes over when you step back?"

The answer sorts the list in ten seconds. If he names a son or a daughter, thank him and move on. If he pauses, or says "that's the problem," you have a listing agreement in front of you within the year. Issac's 8-step sales process currently opens with SDE, timeline and goals — this question is nowhere in it. Add it as step zero.

04

What this changes in the build

Four things, all cheap.
  • Succession becomes a scored field, not a note. The sourcing and scoring system due for approval on 31 August should carry a succession column with three states — heir seated / unknown / no heir on record — built from officer names, company history pages and matching surnames. It is the highest-value field on the whole record and it costs nothing extra to populate.
  • "Who takes over when you step back?" goes into the qualification script as question one. It is a ten-second disqualifier that saves months of pipeline. It also happens to be the question that makes Issac sound like a broker who has done this before.
  • Deal-size banding gets scored too. Anything above roughly $10M should be flagged as a long-cycle name and worked separately, not dropped into the same sequence as a $2M shop. They are different conversations with different competitors.
  • Garland Steel is the template for a phone lane. Roughly one in five of these owner-operator companies publishes no email at all. That is not a gap in the data — it is a segment. Build the CRM so a "no verified email, phone-first" record is a routing decision, not a dead row.
One line to keep in your head

The property filter tells you whether he can sign. The succession answer tells you whether he ever will. The list so far has been built entirely on the first one.
