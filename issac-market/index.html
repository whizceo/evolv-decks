Part one

The finding

Six things the research turned up that change what we do next. Everything after this part is the evidence for them.

The question was who the best brokers are. The useful answer turned out to be narrower than that, and better. There is a published, audited scoreboard for exactly Issac's deal band, and he is not on it. There is a Dallas firm sitting eighth in the country, twenty minutes from his office, with two hundred and twenty-three closed deals against his twelve. And the entire LinkedIn scene he would be competing against for attention is, measured honestly, almost empty.

DATA

There is a scoreboard, and it is the right one

Axial publishes a semi-annual league table for the sub-$10M M&A market and ranks firms on four measured factors, not on reputation. Issac's deals are $5M–$10M. This is his band precisely. He already has an Axial account and has never been ranked on it.

DATA

The strongest competitor is in his own metro

IAG M&A Advisors sits at number eight in the United States and is headquartered in Addison, Texas — inside DFW. It carries 223 total closed deals on Axial. The national leaders are not far away and abstract. One of them is local.

DATA

The national brands are already in Dallas, wearing local names

Transworld and Sunbelt rank third and fourth nationally, and both operate DFW franchises — Transworld North DFW, Sunbelt Dallas Metro. Issac is not competing with independents. He is competing with franchise systems that have national buyer networks and local signage.

DATA

The content field is genuinely, verifiably empty

We measured the last five posts of the credentialled seller-side advisors on LinkedIn. A CEPA with 7,059 followers, a best-selling book and a top-2.5% podcast is getting two to fifteen reactions a post. One of twelve advisors in Texas holding both the CBI and the M&AMI is getting one to four. Top of this market is roughly forty to eighty reactions.

DATA

Credentials do not convert into attention. Stories do

In the whole sample, only two things outperformed: a specific named deal story with a real person in it, and a single sentence naming the owner's own procrastination. Both are about one identifiable human being. Every explainer, every regulatory update, every credential post underperformed the author's own baseline.

DATA

Issac is already in the audience he needs to lead

While reading Jessica Fialkovich's activity, LinkedIn surfaced the reactor list on her strongest post: Issac Newton and forty-six others. He already follows the person doing this best. He is consuming the category he should be defining.

The one-line version. The seller-side of this industry has authority without attention. Every advisor worth naming has the credentials and none of them have the audience. That is not a warning about the channel — it is the entire opportunity, and it is why the hook test is the right first move rather than a clever one.

Part two

How the best are measured

There is a published league table for exactly Issac's deal band, and the four things it grades are worth more than the ranking itself.

Axial runs a marketplace connecting sell-side advisors to acquirers, so it sees the whole process — not just the closes. That is why its league table is worth more than an awards list: every factor is a behaviour the platform observes.

  1. Deal attractiveness
    Pursuits per deal, and total recommended buyers. Measures whether the thing you brought to market is something buyers actually want. A judgement on your selection, before any of your selling.
  2. Buyside targeting effectiveness
    Pursuit rate per deal. Measures whether you sent the deal to the right buyers. A judgement on your list, not your effort.
  3. M&A process effectiveness
    The sell-side ability to advance a buyer from initial pursuit, to NDA, to CIM. Measures whether your process converts interest into commitment.
  4. Deal competitiveness and outcome
    LOI rates and close rates. The only backward-looking factor, and deliberately the last of four.

Read that list again as a diagnosis of February. Issac's Feb/Mar seller campaign produced twenty to thirty percent opens and zero closes, and he concluded the list was wrong rather than the channel. Axial's first two factors — deal attractiveness and buyside targeting — say the same thing in the industry's own language: in the lower middle market, selection is the skill. Two of the four things the best firms are graded on happen before anyone is contacted.

Part four

The strategies, decomposed

What the ranked firms actually do, and what each one is worth to a one-man operation in Texas.

Five strategies show up repeatedly among the firms that rank. They are listed here in the order of how much they are worth to Issac specifically — which is not the order of how impressive they look.

Strategy 01DATA

Selection over volume

Two of Axial's four ranking factors are decided before outreach begins: is this deal attractive, and did you take it to the right buyers. The ranked firms turn down listings. Transworld, Sunbelt and IAG all publish minimum criteria.

This is the February lesson with a scoreboard attached. Issac's floor is already $2M. The strategic move is to make the floor public — a stated minimum is a positioning asset, not an apology.
Strategy 02DATA

Educate the seller years before the sale

The strongest seller-side operators do not run listing ads. They run education. Jessica Fialkovich built Exit Factor — a whole second company — to teach owners how the sale process works. John Warrillow built the Value Builder System and a podcast around one idea: a business that cannot run without you cannot be sold. Laurie Barkman wrote the book and hosts the podcast.

Every one of them monetises a two-to-five-year education runway, because the owner who is ready today already has three brokers calling. Issac's ICP is 'intending to sell in 6–12 months', which is the crowded end. The content strategy is how he reaches them at eighteen months instead.
Strategy 03DATA

Give away the valuation

Axial runs a public valuation calculator. Business Exits publishes multiple data. Warrillow's Value Builder gives an owner a free estimate plus a roadmap. The valuation is the single highest-intent thing an owner will raise their hand for, and the leaders all give it away.

This is the strongest untaken position available to Issac, and it is already in his own psychographic: 78% of owners have never had a formal valuation. A Texas-specific, honest valuation conversation is a better lead magnet than anything in the category — and it is the natural phase 2 offer.
Strategy 04DATA

Franchise the local presence

Transworld and Sunbelt rank third and fourth nationally by being everywhere locally. Between them they hold Transworld Texas, Transworld North DFW, Transworld Central Texas, Transworld South & West Texas, Sunbelt Dallas Metro and Sunbelt Austin.

Issac cannot out-scale this and should not try. But a franchisee is a brand-compliant generalist with a marketing template. What they structurally cannot be is a specific person with a specific opinion about a specific Texas street. That is the gap the content strategy walks into.
Strategy 05READ

Own a category name

The memorable operators own a phrase, not a service. 'The Business Transition Sherpa'. 'Buy Then Build'. 'Boring businesses'. 'The Stewardship Exit'. The phrase does the positioning while they sleep.

Worth noting and worth not rushing. A category name invented before the hook test has run is a guess. Run the forty-eight posts, find which of the twelve territories actually holds, and name that. The test is how you earn the phrase.

The audience trap, stated plainly. Almost all the loud content in this space — Codie Sanchez, Walker Deibel, Elliott Holland — farms people who want to buy a business. That is the biggest audience and it is the wrong one. Issac's buyer flow is already sufficient; his bottleneck is sellers. Copying the loud accounts would grow the wrong number impressively. Take their mechanics — the specificity, the named person, the concrete sum of money — and point them at an owner who is not sure he wants to sell yet.

What the numbers actually teach

  1. Forty to eighty reactions is winning
    Set the expectation now. The Owner Reach instrument already ignores raw engagement in favour of owner-seniority share, which is the right call — but Issac will see the raw number first and it will look small. It is not small. It is top of market.
  2. The credential post is the worst-performing format in the niche
    Every advisor in the sample who led with designations underperformed. The psychographic already says the ICP does not know what a CBI is. The data now agrees.
  3. One identifiable human being, every time
    The two posts that beat their own baselines both had a specific person in them. Not 'business owners'. A man in Brownsville. An owner saying 'maybe one more year'.
  4. The regulatory explainer is a trap
    Barkman's SBA rule-change post is genuinely useful, well written, and got seven reactions. Usefulness is not the variable. Recognition is.
  5. Personal posts work here and are not off-limits
    Fiore's monthly life-stats post pulled 24 reactions and 5 comments — above his own explainer baseline. Worth one slot, not more, and only after the hook test has a baseline to compare against.

Part nine

The honest limits

What this research does not establish, stated before anyone acts on it.

  • The LinkedIn sample is five posts per person — Enough to establish an order of magnitude, not enough to rank individuals. 'Barkman gets fewer reactions than Fialkovich' is safe. 'Barkman's content is worse' is not supported.
  • Axial only sees Axial — The league table measures deals that ran through one marketplace. A firm doing well off-platform is invisible to it. IAG's 223 is real and comparable; its absence would not have proved anything.
  • No commission or revenue figures for any competitor — None of these firms publish them and we did not estimate. Deal counts are the only comparable number and they are counts, not sizes.
  • Reactions are not opportunities — We measured attention, not revenue. Issac's belief that small creators are getting real deals from LinkedIn is plausible and consistent with what we saw — his own two leads from one post are the strongest evidence we hold — but no public number proves it for anyone else.
  • The competitor set is Axial-active firms — A Texas broker who never touches the platform is not in the 93. For the DFW $5M–$10M band this is a good proxy, not a census.

What happens next

  1. Nothing here changes the hook test
    The forty-eight posts run as designed. Two territories got evidence, one format got ruled out. That is a refinement, not a redirection.
  2. The valuation offer is the phase 2 candidate
    It is the highest-intent thing an owner will ask for, the leaders all give it away, and 78% of his ICP has never had one. It should be built the day the hook test names a winning territory.
  3. The reactor lists are the first list to build by hand
    Owners who reacted to seller-side exit content in Texas. Small, warm, and available today without a single export.
  4. Ask Issac one question
    He already follows Fialkovich. Ask him what he thinks of her content and why he follows her — the answer is worth more than another week of research, and it is the fastest read available on the voice he actually admires.
