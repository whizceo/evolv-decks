Who the account reached
82%
102,121 of the 124,731 people reached were reached about MS — the segment with no FDA indication, where the patient pays $2–7K in cash, and where CytoKind has never made a single sale.
Who actually landed on a page
92%
8,495 of 9,207 landing-page views were MS. Derm — the segment that produced all six sales ever, where insurance pays and the patient pays nothing — got 712 of them.
Why it drifted there
$0.19
MS traffic is the cheapest thing in the account, against $0.30–$0.51 for derm. An account optimised on cost per view will walk toward the audience that cannot buy. That is not a targeting mistake, it is the objective doing its job.
Three findings, none of which need a budget figure. One — the account has spent the year talking to the wrong segment, and the proof is in people, not dollars: 82% of everyone reached, 92% of everyone who landed. Two — 9,207 landing-page views in one month against a CRM that holds 334 records in total means the page converts somewhere well under 3%, where this model has to assume 12%; traffic was never the constraint. Three — every reporting campaign in that window is now ended, all four on 8 August, which is the failed card, and the seven ads live today were relaunched on the 11th with almost none of this history carried over.
The one thing to ask Tom for, and it is a screenshot, not a project: the Amount-spent column for the same window, plus results by campaign for the ads live now. That single image turns cost per contact, cost per JotForm, CAC and ROAS from blanks into numbers — and it is the only reason the spend lever exists on this page at all.

Cost per click is unreported for all five, which is itself the finding: nobody is bidding on them.
Not one of the five terms names a disease and a treatment in the same breath. Two of them — home-made bulbs and neonatal jaundice — describe people who can never buy this product. One is the supplier's own brand name. There is no organic demand capture for “psoriasis light therapy at home”, “does insurance cover phototherapy”, or “vitiligo home treatment”, which are the searches an indicated, insured, closeable patient actually makes. The site ranks for the device and not for the problem.
And the button the whole homepage is built around is counted nowhere. The primary call to action on cytokind.net is “Book a 10-Min Discovery Call”, with “Explore the Science” beside it. There is no booking log, no calendar report, no show-rate figure in any system — so the site's main conversion event has never been observed once. It is a lever in section 03, set to zero and tagged N.
One comparison I am deliberately not making. 5.1K desktop visits a month and 9,207 landing-page views in the July ad window are not the same measurement and must not be netted off against each other — one is a third-party desktop-only estimate, the other is platform-reported and includes mobile, which is where nearly all of this traffic lives. Both are here because they point the same way. Neither is evidence about the other.

The content scoreboard

All 18 organic posts on @cytokind_inc · 10 Sep 2025 → 6 Aug 2026 · real numbers

This is not a sample. It is everything CytoKind has published organically, with the real view count against each hook. Ten videos, eight stills, 331 days. The verdict column compares each post to the account's own average rather than to an industry number, because the only honest benchmark here is itself.
Benchmark views · the account's own average Verdict fires above 100 views. Stills have no view count and are marked accordingly.
The best post this account has ever made is the one pointing at the asset that closes. 324 views — an interview clip about a patient going from a fatigue score of 3 to 1 — is the all-time ceiling, and it sits directly upstream of the article John closes roughly one in three readers on. The second and third best are a researcher explaining what narrowband UVB is (294) and a patient saying her energy came back (248). Explainers and testimonials work; press mentions do not — the eight stills average 2.5 likes between them, and the Economist link got zero. Two of the ten videos are the same file posted twice, and both times the repost did worse than the original.
And a collision I am putting on the page rather than quietly fixing. The returns document values a “content 10×” at roughly +$1,600/month, built on a baseline of 8 posts a month at 900 views each. The real account publishes 1.6 posts a month at 202 views — so that baseline is overstated by about 22×, and the increment computed from it is not reliable. The correct reading is not that content matters less; it is that content has effectively never been run, and its true baseline is close to zero. That is a bigger opportunity and a smaller certainty at the same time, and it should be presented that way.
